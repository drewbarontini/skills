# Shape Writing Spine

Turn an emerging idea, lived observation, Signal, Inquiry, or collection of thoughts into a Core Idea and Three Claims connected by explicit transitions. Discover the author's argument through conversation; preserve their thinking and language rather than supplying a polished thesis prematurely.

## Inputs and Boundaries

Start with the author's observations, prior conversations, or existing Work. Read a supplied, accessible Work or Inquiry before interviewing. Do not invent experiences, evidence, claims, or quotations.

This Skill shapes an argument rather than drafting the piece or conducting an investigation. Frame Inquiry is a separate job when the purpose is to investigate uncertainty. Analogy Builder can optionally help reveal or test a relationship; an analogy is not a required stage or output.

## Method

1. **Surface the idea.** Invite an actual observation, experience, unresolved tension, or surprising connection. Ask what the author noticed and why it matters rather than demanding a title or thesis. Reflect the account and distinguish the observation from your interpretation.
2. **Connect the claims.** Draw out the implication of the observation, what it changes in the author's understanding, and what the reader needs to understand next. Elicit approximately three declarative claims. Test both transitions: if the first claim is true, why does the second follow? What remains unresolved that makes the third necessary? Probe assumptions, counterexamples, or missing links where they change the argument.
3. **Compress the spine.** Once the reasoning is clear and the author is ready, propose a working name, a two- or three-sentence Core Idea, and three connected claims. Explain each claim briefly and include a transition after claims one and two. Refine from feedback without expanding into an outline or draft.

## Interview Behavior

- Ask one meaningful question at a time, then follow the answer rather than running a fixed questionnaire.
- Reflect tentative claims in the author's language and distinguish a possible claim from a confirmed position.
- Use offered stories, examples, and analogies to investigate the idea without making them mandatory output sections.
- Challenge gaps respectfully. Do not manufacture continuity to fill three slots. Three is the default; a weak third claim calls for investigation or simplification.
- When the throughline is clear, present the spine rather than prolonging the interview. Preserve an existing working name if it remains useful.

If a claim or transition is unsettled, state the gap and ask the most useful next question. An incomplete argument does not justify a finished-looking spine.

## Output

Use the compact Writing Spine template below. Do not add mandatory sections for evidence, stories, analogies, paragraphs, or a drafting plan.

```markdown
# [Working Name]

## Core Idea
[Two or three sentences capturing the central idea and why it matters.]

## Three Claims

### 1. [First declarative claim.]
[One or two sentences explaining the premise.]
**Transition →** [Why the next claim is necessary.]

### 2. [Second declarative claim.]
[One or two sentences developing the argument.]
**Transition →** [The tension or consequence leading to the final claim.]

### 3. [Third declarative claim.]
[One or two sentences expressing the resulting insight or implication.]
```

For a Notion Work, its existing Name property supplies the working title; do not duplicate it in the body. Preserve other properties, relations, and prose. Save to Notion only when requested, and do not overwrite a published Work with this working scaffold.

## Quality Bar

A strong spine expresses the author's actual thinking, states specific claims as sentences, makes both logical transitions visible, and reads as one coherent argument. It remains shorter than an outline and leaves prose, stories, examples, and analogies for drafting. Preserve uncertainty rather than claiming the interview established more than it did.
